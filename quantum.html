Where the world goes weird

— an opening note at the edge of the very small —
The Big Idea in one breath

Quantum mechanics is the mathematical framework that describes nature at its smallest scales — atoms, electrons, photons. At that scale, the classical intuitions forged in a macroscopic world of billiard balls and clocks collapse. Energy arrives in packets. A particle can be in many states at once. Two particles across a galaxy can behave as a single system.

The theory is not a metaphor or a philosophical mood — it is the most precisely tested theory in the history of science. Its predictions have been confirmed to better than ten parts in a billion. Every smartphone, GPS satellite, laser and MRI scanner runs on effects that are quantum at heart.

1. Granularity
Physical quantities like energy are quantized — they come in indivisible chunks known as quanta.
2. Probability
Quantum theory predicts probabilities of outcomes, not certainties. The world has room for chance.
3. Superposition
Before measurement, a system can exist as a blend of many states, like a coin spinning before it lands.
4. Correlation
Particles can become entangled, sharing a fate that no distance can separate.

The Quanta of Energy

— Max Planck starts a quiet revolution, 1900 —
Black-body radiation & the ultraviolet catastrophe

Physicists at the turn of the century were trying to explain the spectrum of light emitted by a heated object. Classical physics predicted that a hot object should radiate an infinite amount of energy at very short wavelengths — an absurdity known as the ultraviolet catastrophe.

In 1900, Max Planck made a desperate but brilliant assumption: the energy of an oscillator is not continuous, but comes in discrete packets. He proposed that energy E is an integer multiple of a fundamental unit:

E = n·h·f

where f is the frequency, n is a whole number, and h is a new constant of nature — Planck's constant, roughly 6.626 × 10−34 joule-seconds. Its minuscule size is why the world seems smooth: the "steps" of energy are far too tiny to notice at everyday scales, yet are the entire basis of physics at the atomic scale.

The constant that hides everywhere
"An expert is a person who has made all the mistakes that can be made in a very narrow field." — this constant h quietly governs everything.
Planck called his own idea "an act of despair… I knew the problem was of fundamental importance, and knew the formula for energy distribution; a theoretical interpretation had to be found at any cost."

The Photoelectric Effect

— Einstein turns a packet into a particle of light, 1905 —
Light knocks out electrons

Shine light on a metal and electrons are ejected. Classical wave theory said the intensity (brightness) should determine the energy of the ejected electrons. Experiments showed the opposite: the energy of ejected electrons depends only on the frequency (colour) of the light, not its brightness. Below a threshold frequency, no electrons are ejected at all — no matter how intense the light.

In 1905, Einstein explained this by proposing that light itself is made of individual packets of energy — particles we now call photons. Each photon carries energy E = h·f. One photon is absorbed by one electron. If the photon's energy exceeds the metal's "work function" φ, the electron is freed with the remainder as kinetic energy:

Ek = h·f − φ

This single insight — that light behaves as a stream of particles — earned Einstein the Nobel Prize (not relativity!) and seeded the entire quantum revolution. It shatters the neat picture of light as a continuous wave.

Wave prediction: brighter light ⇒ more energetic electrons. Wrong.
Photon reality: bluer (higher-f) light ⇒ more energetic electrons. ✓ Correct.

Wave–Particle Duality

— is it a wave, or is it a particle? Yes. —
The two-slit heart of quantum weirdness
Fire electrons — not light — one at a time through two slits.
Each electron lands as a crisp particle dot. But collect enough dots and they build an interference pattern — as if every electron "knew" about both slits and interfered with itself.
The double-slit experiment

The most profound demonstration in physics: send particles (or single photons) through two slits. With both slits open, you get an interference pattern of bright and dark bands — the signature of a wave. Close one slit and the pattern disappears into a simple blob.

If you try to detect which slit each particle passes, the interference pattern vanishes. The act of measurement forces the particle to "choose" a path, collapsing the delicate wavelike behaviour.

The conclusion: a quantum object is not purely a particle and not purely a wave — it is a third thing described by a wavefunction whose behaviour only appears particle-like or wave-like depending on how we look at it.

"Everything we call real is made of things that cannot be regarded as real." — Niels Bohr

The Wavefunction

— Erwin Schrödinger's masterpiece, 1926 —
ψ — the equation that rules the quantum world

Erwin Schrödinger asked: if particles have wavelike nature, what is the wave? He wrote down an equation whose solution — the wavefunction ψ (psi) — completely describes the quantum state of a system. For a particle of mass m moving in a potential V:

solutions in a box (the particle can only take certain energies)

iħ ∂ψ/∂t = −(ħ²/2m) ∂²ψ/∂x² + V·ψ

This is the time-dependent Schrödinger equation. It looks like a wave equation — but its meaning is stranger. The wavefunction itself is not directly observable. What is observable is the probability density: the chance of finding the particle at a location is proportional to the square of the wavefunction's magnitude, |ψ|².

Born's rule (1926): the probability of a measurement outcome is |ψ|2. This is the single most important "recipe" in quantum theory — and one of its most debated.

Stationary states
Some solutions have a definite energy and don't change with time — the "allowed" energy levels of an atom.
Normalisation
The total probability of finding the particle somewhere must be 1, so the wavefunction is "normalised" — the particle has to be somewhere.
Linearity
If ψ₁ and ψ₂ are solutions, so is any sum of them. This linearity is the root of superposition.

The Uncertainty Principle

— Werner Heisenberg, 1927: knowledge has a price —
You can't know everything at once

Heisenberg discovered that certain pairs of observables cannot both be known with arbitrary precision. The more precisely you know a particle's position x, the less precisely you can know its momentum p, and vice versa:

Δx · Δp ≥ ħ/2

Here Δx is the uncertainty in position, Δp the uncertainty in momentum, and ħ (h-bar) is Planck's constant divided by 2π. Similarly, energy and time obey ΔE·Δt ≥ ħ/2.

Importantly, this is not about imperfect instruments. It is a fundamental limit built into nature. An electron's position and momentum simply do not both have definite values at the same time — the sharper you make one, the fuzzier the other becomes.

The uncertainty principle is why atoms exist at all — electrons can't fall all the way into the nucleus.

position vs momentum localise
Why atoms are stable
If you confine an electron tightly (small Δx), its momentum — and therefore energy — skyrockets (large Δp). Squeezing it any closer costs enormous energy. The atom settles at a minimum-energy "ground state" that refuses to collapse.

Superposition & Quantum States

— a particle can hold many possibilities at once —
The choreography of possibilities

Because the wavefunction is linear, a system can exist as a superposition — a coherent blend — of many states. A "qubit" (quantum bit) can be in the state |0⟩, the state |1⟩, or — here's the magic — a combination of both:

|ψ⟩ = a·|0⟩ + b·|1⟩

where a and b are complex amplitudes whose squares give the probabilities of measuring 0 or 1 (with |a|² + |b|² = 1). The electron is not "in both states" in the way a spinning coin is "sort of" heads and tails — it is in a genuinely indefinite state that the mathematics captures perfectly.

Schrödinger illustrated the discomfort of this idea with a thought experiment: a cat in a box whose life depends on a quantum decay. Until we look, the cat is — as the naive reading of the equation suggests — a superposition of alive and dead.

The Bloch sphere

Any single-qubit state can be visualised as a point on the surface of a sphere. The poles represent the "classical" states |0⟩ and |1⟩; every other point on the sphere is a distinct quantum superposition with its own phase.

The arrow (state vector) points wherever the qubit "is" between measurements. A quantum computer nudges this arrow with quantum gates, exploring an enormous space of possibilities simultaneously.

With n qubits, a register can represent 2ⁿ numbers at once — exponential states, linear hardware.

Quantum Tunneling

— passing through walls that physics says are impassable —
Through the barrier, not over it

Classically, a ball cannot roll over an energy barrier higher than its own energy. Quantum mechanically, the wavefunction does not drop to zero at a barrier — it leaks through. There is a small but real probability that the particle appears on the far side, as if it "tunnelled" through the wall.

The probability of tunneling falls off exponentially with the barrier's height and width. For macroscopic objects the probability is effectively zero, but for electrons it is not just real — it's essential.

Nuclear fusion
The Sun fuses protons only because quantum tunneling lets them overcome their electrostatic repulsion.
Scanning microscopes
STM images surfaces atom-by-atom by measuring the tunneling current between tip and sample.
Flash memory & diodes
Tunneling is engineered into flash storage and tunnel diodes you use every day.

Spin & the Pauli Principle

— an intrinsic property that makes chemistry possible —
Particles have a hidden "spinning" degree of freedom

Every elementary particle carries an intrinsic angular momentum called spin, which is inherently quantum. Fermions (electrons, protons, quarks) have half-integer spin (½, 3/2…); bosons (photons, gluons) have integer spin (0, 1, 2…). An electron's spin is always a fixed magnitude — it can be up or down, and nothing in between along any chosen axis.

Wolfgang Pauli's exclusion principle states that no two identical fermions can occupy the same quantum state. This single rule is why electrons in an atom stack into distinct shells instead of collapsing onto one another — it is the structural backbone of chemistry, and it is why ordinary matter has volume at all.

No two electrons can share the same set of quantum numbers

Bosons, by contrast, love to crowd together. The same rule that separates electrons lets trillions of photons share a single laser beam, and allows matter to form a Bose–Einstein condensate — a cloud of atoms behaving as one macroscopic quantum object.

Entanglement & Bell's Theorem

— particles whose fates are woven together across any distance —
"Spooky action at a distance"

If two particles interact, they can become entangled: their quantum states become a single, inseparable whole. Measuring one immediately determines the outcome of measuring the other — even if they are light-years apart.

Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen (EPR, 1935) argued this must mean quantum mechanics was incomplete — surely the particles carried "hidden" instructions. In the 1960s, John Bell derived an inequality that distinguishes between any theory of local hidden variables and genuine quantum entanglement. Experiments (beginning with Alain Aspect in 1982) violated Bell's inequality.

The verdict is unambiguous: nature is non-local. Entangled particles really are interconnected in a way that no classical local theory can reproduce. (It does not allow faster-than-light communication — no usable information travels between them.)

"God does not play dice." — Einstein.  “Stop telling God what to do.” — Bohr.

Bell's inequality (1964)
Bell showed that local hidden-variable theories predict certain statistical bounds that quantum mechanics violates. Experiments side with quantum mechanics every time.

Interpretations

— the equations agree; the meaning is contested —
Copenhagen
Bohr & Heisenberg: the wavefunction is a tool for predicting measurements; it "collapses" on observation. Don't ask what's really there between measurements.
Many-Worlds
Everett: no collapse at all — every possible outcome happens, in branching parallel universes. We just see the branch we're on.
Pilot-Wave (de Broglie–Bohm)
A deterministic hidden variable: a real particle is guided by a pilot wave. Non-local, but fully deterministic.
QBism
Quantum states are about an agent's beliefs and expectations, not objective reality. "Quantum" is a tool for making better bets.
Objective Collapse
Wavefunctions genuinely collapse due to a physical process, especially for large systems — a bridge between quantum and classical.
The Takeaway
All interpretations predict identical observable results today. The question of "what it all means" remains one of science's deepest open mysteries.

Quantum Computing

— computing with superposed possibilities —
Qubits, gates, and exponential power

A classical bit is 0 or 1. A qubit, via superposition, can be a blend of both — and entangled qubits form a space whose dimension grows exponentially with their number. Algorithms like Shor's (factoring integers) and Grover's (searching an unsorted database) exploit this to achieve speeds impossible on any classical machine.

The catch is decoherence: qubits are fragile, and any interaction with the environment destroys their superposition (this is, essentially, quantum measurement). Building a working large-scale quantum computer means isolating qubits near absolute zero while steering them with exquisite precision.

quantum supremacy · error correction · logical qubits · the frontier

As of today, quantum computers have demonstrated "quantum advantage" on narrow tasks but are not yet broadly useful due to noise. The race is on to build fault-tolerant machines that could crack certain cryptographic schemes and simulate molecules exactly — and to build quantum-safe cryptography in parallel.

Quantum in the Real World

— you are using it right now —
Lasers
Dental tools, barcode scanners, optical discs, fibre-optic internet — all rest on stimulated emission, a purely quantum process.
Semiconductors & chips
Transistors exploit electron behaviour described by quantum band theory. The entire digital age is quantum under the hood.
MRI & medical imaging
Magnetic resonance imaging reads the quantum spin of protons in your body to build 3-D images of soft tissue.
GPS & atomic clocks
Atomic clocks measure transitions between quantum energy levels — the heart of satellite navigation and global time.
Quantum cryptography
QKD uses entanglement to distribute keys in a way that any eavesdropper unavoidably disturbs — detectable and uncrackable.
Solar cells
The photovoltaic effect is the photoelectric effect in reverse: absorbing a photon promotes an electron across a quantum gap.

The Scientists & Their Contributions

— the people who tore open the very small —
Meet the revolutionaries

Quantum mechanics is not a single discovery but a generation of breathtaking ideas, each pinned to a person who risked the impossible. These are the men — and the one monumental thought each of them contributed to the whole.

1879–1955the photoelectron
Albert Einstein
The Photoelectric Effect · Nobel 1921

Proposed that light is made of discrete photons, each carrying energy E = h·f — the key that unlocked particle-like light and helped launch quantum theory (even though he later resisted its probabilities).

With Podolsky & Rosen he framed the EPR paradox, the seed of the entanglement debate.

"God does not play dice."
1858–1947the quantizer
Max Planck
Energy Quanta · Nobel 1918

Rescued physics from the ultraviolet catastrophe by proposing that energy is emitted and absorbed in discrete packets — quanta — governed by the constant h that now bears his name.

He called his own stroke of genius "an act of despair".

"A new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its opponents."
1885–1962the atom-builder
Niels Bohr
Quantum Atomic Model · Nobel 1922

Built the first successful quantum model of the atom, with electrons in discrete energy orbits that jump and radiate — explaining the hydrogen spectral lines.

As the father of the Copenhagen interpretation, he argued that measurement plays an irreducible role in reality.

"There is no quantum world. There is only an abstract quantum physical description."
1901–1976the uncertain one
Werner Heisenberg
Uncertainty Principle · Nobel 1932

Developed matrix mechanics, the first complete formulation of quantum theory, and then proved that position and momentum cannot both be known precisely — Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2.

His principle set a fundamental limit on knowledge itself, not on our instruments.

"What we observe is not nature itself, but nature exposed to our method of questioning."
1887–1961the wave-writer
Erwin Schrödinger
Wave Equation · Nobel 1933

Wrote the fundamental wave equation whose solutions, the wavefunctions ψ, describe all of quantum behaviour — elegantly bridging wave and particle.

His famous cat remains the world's best-known icon of superposition and the measurement problem.

"The task is not so much to see what no one has yet seen."
1902–1984the theorist's theorist
Paul Dirac
Relativistic QM & Antimatter · Nobel 1933

Merged quantum mechanics with special relativity. His famous Dirac equation predicted antimatter — a particle (the positron) was later found, confirming his pure mathematics.

He also created the bra–ket notation every physicist now uses.

"The fundamental laws necessary for the mathematical treatment of a large part of physics are completely known…"
1882–1970the rule-maker
Max Born
Born's Rule · Nobel 1954

Gave the wavefunction its meaning: the square of its magnitude, |ψ|², is the probability of finding the particle there. This probabilistic interpretation — Born's rule — is the very heart of the theory.

He also mentored many of the field's giants at Göttingen.

"The belief that there is only one truth… is the root of all evil."
1892–1987the matter-wave man
Louis de Broglie
Matter Waves · Nobel 1929

Dared to propose that particles have waves — that every object, from an electron to a baseball, carries a wavelength λ = h/p.

Electron waves were soon confirmed experimentally, giving the whole framework its duality.

"If matter has a wave nature, waves must have a particle nature."
1928–1990the reality-tester
John Stewart Bell
Bell's Theorem

Proved through Bell's inequality that any local, hidden-variable theory must differ from quantum mechanics — turning the debate over entanglement into a testable experiment.

Every Bell test since has sided with quantum mechanics: the world is genuinely non-local.

"Bohr was inconsistent, unclear, obscure, and right. Einstein was consistent, clear, down-to-earth, and wrong."

Glossary

— the vocabulary of the very small —
Quantum

A discrete unit, or packet, of any physical quantity — "the least amount".

Photon

The quantum of light; a massless particle carrying energy E = h·f.

Wavefunction ψ

The mathematical object encoding everything knowable about a quantum system.

Qubit

The quantum analogue of a bit, existing in a superposition of 0 and 1.

Superposition

The coexistence of multiple states until measurement.

Entanglement

A correlation between particles that no distance can sever.

Decoherence

Loss of superposition due to interaction with the environment — the great foe of quantum computers.

Hermitian observable

A physical quantity (position, momentum, spin) represented by an operator whose eigenvalues are the possible measurement results.

Planck constant h

The fundamental scale of quantum mechanics, ≈ 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s.
