Malus domestica— Nature's Perfect Fruit —

A Fruit That Shaped Civilizations

The apple, Malus domestica, is far more than a simple snack. It is a fruit woven into the very fabric of human history — from the mythical gardens of antiquity to the rolling orchards of modern agriculture. With its crisp bite, fragrant aroma, and dazzling spectrum of colors, the apple has captivated humanity for millennia.

Today, apples are cultivated on every inhabited continent. Farmers and scientists have developed thousands of cultivars, each with its own unique flavor profile, texture, and appearance. Whether tart, sweet, or somewhere in between, there is an apple for every palate and every purpose — from fresh eating to baking, juicing, and fermentation.

The story of the apple is a story of migration, innovation, and cultural exchange. It has traveled the Silk Road, crossed oceans, and been planted by emperors and pioneers alike. To understand the apple is to understand a significant thread in the tapestry of human civilization itself.

Origin & History

A journey through millions of years, from wild forests to the fruit bowls of today.

~50 Million Years Ago

Ancient Ancestors

Fossil evidence suggests that the earliest ancestors of the modern apple originated in the forests of what is now Central Asia during the Eocene epoch. These primitive fruits were small, bitter, and bore little resemblance to the apples we know today, but they carried the genetic blueprint that would eventually give rise to one of the world's most important food crops.

~20,000 Years Ago

The Tian Shan Forests

Deep in the Tian Shan mountain range of modern-day Kazakhstan, wild apple forests flourished. Malus sieversii, the primary ancestor of the domestic apple, grew in remarkable genetic diversity here. These wild apples varied enormously in size, color, and flavor — a genetic reservoir that modern breeders still draw from today. The village of Ararat, Kazakhstan, is home to the Apple Tree of Semenko, believed to be around 550 years old.

~3000 BCE

The Silk Road

As trade routes developed across Central Asia, apples began their westward migration along the Silk Road. Merchants carried apple seeds and cuttings along the same paths that transported silk, spices, and ideas. The fruit gradually spread through Persia, Mesopotamia, and into the Mediterranean basin, adapting to new climates and soils along the way.

~1000 BCE

Greece & Rome

Ancient Greeks and Romans embraced the apple with enthusiasm. The Greeks associated apples with Aphrodite, the goddess of love, and threw apples at weddings as symbols of fertility. The Romans, under the influence of writers like Pliny the Elder, catalogued dozens of apple varieties and developed sophisticated orchard cultivation techniques. Roman legions spread apple cultivation throughout their empire, planting orchards across Britain, Gaul, and Germania.

~800 CE

Charlemagne's Orchards

Emperor Charlemagne decreed that apples should be planted on all imperial estates. His famous agricultural directive, the Capitulare de Villis, listed apple among the essential fruits to be cultivated. Benedictine monks became master orchardists, preserving and propagating apple varieties throughout medieval Europe. Many of the cider apple varieties still grown in Normandy and Brittany trace their lineage to monastic plantings of this era.

1600s–1700s

Colonial America

European settlers brought apple seeds and cuttings to North America, where the fruit found ideal growing conditions. Johnny Appleseed — real name John Chapman — became a legendary figure, planting nurseries across Pennsylvania, Ohio, and Indiana in the early 1800s. Almost every colonial homestead had an apple tree, used for cider, cooking, and preservation. Apples were so central to colonial life that cider was the beverage of choice for men, women, and children alike.

1866

The Red Delicious

In Peru, Iowa, a seedling was discovered by Jesse Hiatt that would eventually become the Red Delicious apple. After years of cultivation and promotion by the Stark Brothers Nursery, it became the most widely grown apple in the United States by the mid-20th century. Though its reign eventually gave way to tastier cultivars, the Red Delicious demonstrated the power of marketing in the fruit industry and shaped American apple consumption for generations.

1966

The Honeycrisp Revolution

Developed at the University of Minnesota by crossbreeding the Macoun and Honeygold varieties, the Honeycrisp apple was released in 1991 after decades of careful selection. Its extraordinary crispness and balanced sweet-tart flavor transformed the premium apple market. The Honeycrisp's success ushered in an era of flavor-focused breeding and proved that consumers would pay a premium for a genuinely superior apple experience.

Beloved Varieties

Each apple cultivar tells its own story through flavor, texture, and heritage.

🍏

Honeycrisp

Developed at the University of Minnesota and released in 1991, the Honeycrisp has large cells that burst when bitten, creating an extraordinarily juicy and crisp experience. Its balanced sweet-tart flavor profile with notes of honey has made it the gold standard for premium eating apples worldwide. The Honeycrisp took over 30 years to develop and remains one of the most expensive apples at market due to its delicate growing requirements.

Sweet-Tart Extra Crisp Fall Harvest
🍏

Granny Smith

Originating in Australia in 1868, the Granny Smith is the world's most recognizable green apple. Its tart, acidic flavor and firm, dense flesh make it ideal for both fresh eating and cooking. The bright green skin holds up beautifully in pies and tarts, and its longevity in storage is legendary. Named after Maria Ann Smith, who discovered the seedling growing near her orchard in Eastwood, Sydney.

Tart Firm Flesh Versatile
🍏

Fuji

Developed in Fujisaki, Japan in the late 1930s and released in 1962, the Fuji is one of the sweetest apple varieties, with sugar content reaching up to 22%. Its dense, crisp flesh and honeyed sweetness have made it enormously popular in Asian markets and increasingly loved worldwide. The Fuji excels in both fresh eating and as a cooking apple, holding its shape well when heated.

Very Sweet Dense Long Storage
🍏

Gala

First grown in New Zealand in the 1930s by crossing the Golden Delicious and Kidd's Orange Red, the Gala has become one of the world's most commercially successful apples. Its mild, sweet flavor, beautiful orange-red striping, and excellent storage life make it a perennial favorite. Gala apples are particularly well-suended to warm climates and have become the dominant variety in many Southern Hemisphere growing regions.

Mild Sweet Aromatic Popular
🍏

Pink Lady

Bred by John Cripps in Western Australia in the 1970s by crossing Golden Delicious and Lady Williams, the Pink Lady (Cripps Pink) is distinguished by its striking pink-green skin and refreshing tartness. It has a notably effervescent quality when bitten, and requires a long growing season to develop its full complex flavor. This variety requires strict quality standards to carry the Pink Lady brand name.

Tart-Crisp Effervescent Late Season
🍏

Braeburn

Discovered as a chance seedling in the Braeburn Orchard of Motueka, New Zealand in the 1950s, the Braeburn is one of the finest dual-purpose apples. Its complex flavor profile combines spicy, wine-like notes with a rich sweetness and firm, dense flesh that holds up excellently in both fresh preparations and baked dishes. The Braeburn has become a staple of the premium apple market in Europe and North America.

Spicy-Sweet Dual Purpose Aromatic

Anatomy of an Apple

Understanding the intricate structure hidden beneath that familiar skin.

Five Essential Parts

🔴

The Skin (Exocarp)

The outer protective layer of the apple, ranging in color from deep crimson to pale green, golden yellow, and even russeted brown. The skin contains the majority of the apple's fiber and is rich in antioxidants, particularly quercetin and anthocyanins. A waxy coating called the cuticle naturally protects the fruit from moisture loss and pathogens.

🟠

The Flesh (Mesocarp)

The crisp, juicy flesh that we most commonly eat constitutes the bulk of the fruit. Composed of approximately 85% water, the flesh's texture varies dramatically between cultivars — from the dense crunch of a Honeycrisp to the softer bite of a McIntosh. The flesh contains sugars, organic acids, and aromatic compounds that create each variety's distinctive flavor profile.

🟢

The Core (Endocarp)

The central core houses the seed chambers, known as carpels. An apple typically has five carpels arranged in a star pattern when cut crosswise. The core tissue is tougher and more fibrous than the surrounding flesh, and while technically edible, it is usually discarded. The star-shaped core pattern is one of the most recognizable features in botanical illustration.

⚪

The Seeds

Each carpel typically contains one to three dark brown seeds, shaped like teardrops. Apple seeds contain amygdalin, a cyanogenic glycoside that can release small amounts of hydrogen cyanide when crushed or chewed. However, the quantity is so small that swallowing a few whole seeds is harmless. A single apple can contain 5–20 seeds, and each seed carries the genetic potential for an entirely new variety.

🌱

The Stem (Peduncle)

The woody stem connects the apple to its branch and serves as the fruit's lifeline during development, transporting water and nutrients from the tree. The stem's length and thickness vary between cultivars — some apples have long, elegant stems while others have short, stubby ones that sit in a pronounced cavity. After picking, the stem's condition is an indicator of freshness.

Health Benefits

Scientific research has linked regular apple consumption to remarkable health outcomes across multiple systems.

❤️

Heart Health

Regular apple consumption has been linked to a reduced risk of cardiovascular disease. The soluble fiber in apples helps lower blood cholesterol levels, while the polyphenol epicatechin is associated with lower blood pressure. A large-scale study published in JAMA Internal Medicine found that eating one apple a day was associated with a 27% reduction in mortality from cardiovascular disease, making it one of the most impactful dietary choices for cardiac wellness.

🩹

Blood Sugar Regulation

Despite their natural sugar content, apples have a low glycemic index of 36. The high fiber content — particularly pectin — slows sugar absorption, preventing rapid blood glucose spikes. Polyphenols in apples may also improve insulin sensitivity. Research from the Harvard School of Public Health found that women who ate apples regularly had a 28% lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes compared to those who rarely consumed them.

🦠

Gut Health

Apples are rich in pectin, a prebiotic fiber that feeds beneficial gut bacteria. A healthy gut microbiome is essential for digestion, immune function, and even mental health through the gut-brain axis. Studies have shown that the polyphenols in apples can increase populations of beneficial Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus species, contributing to a balanced and thriving gut ecosystem that supports overall wellness.

🛡️

Immune Support

The vitamin C in apples supports the production and function of white blood cells, while the antioxidant quercetin has anti-inflammatory and antihistamine properties. Apple skin contains triterpenoids that stimulate the immune system. Regular apple consumption has been shown to improve immune surveillance and reduce the severity of respiratory infections, particularly during cold and flu season.

🧠

Brain Health

Apple juice has been shown to protect neurons from oxidative damage and may help prevent neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer's disease. Quercetin in apples guards brain cells against free radical attack. Animal studies have demonstrated that apple juice consumption improves memory and cognitive function, and epidemiological data suggests that regular apple eaters have measurably better cognitive performance in later life.

🦴

Bone Health

Studies suggest that the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory compounds in fruits — including apples — may promote bone density and strength. The anti-inflammatory properties of apple polyphenols may reduce bone resorption, while the boron content helps the body retain calcium. These mechanisms make apples a valuable addition to diets aimed at preventing osteoporosis and maintaining skeletal health as we age.

🫁

Lung Health

The flavonoids in apples, particularly quercetin, have been shown to reduce inflammation in the airways and decrease the risk of asthma. A study in the journal Thorax found that people who ate five or more apples per week had significantly better lung function than those who didn't. Apple consumption has also been linked to a reduced risk of lung cancer in several large cohort studies spanning millions of participants.

⚖️

Weight Management

Apples are high in fiber and water content, making them exceptionally filling relative to their calorie count. Research from the journal Appetite found that eating apple slices before a meal reduced total calorie intake by 15%. The pectin in apples swells in the stomach, promoting satiety and reducing overall food consumption — making them an ideal snack for weight-conscious individuals seeking a satisfying yet low-calorie option.

🔬

Cancer Risk Reduction

Several epidemiological studies have found associations between apple consumption and reduced risk of various cancers, including colorectal, breast, and lung cancer. The antioxidants in apples — particularly the triterpenoids in the skin — have demonstrated anti-cancer properties in laboratory studies, including the ability to inhibit cancer cell proliferation and induce apoptosis in tumor cells through multiple pathways.

The Growing Process

From dormant bud to ripe fruit — the annual cycle of an apple tree is a marvel of nature.

1
Winter

Dormancy

During the cold winter months, apple trees enter a state of dormancy. Buds formed the previous summer remain sealed and protected. The tree requires a specific number of "chill hours" — hours between 32°F and 45°F — to properly reset its internal clock. Without sufficient chill hours, bloom will be erratic and poor fruit set results the following season.

2
Spring

Bloom

As temperatures warm in spring, flower buds swell and burst open in spectacular displays of white and pink blossoms. Each flower cluster typically contains five to six individual blossoms. This is the critical pollination window — bees must transfer pollen from one variety to another, as most apple trees are self-incompatible and require cross-pollination to set fruit successfully.

3
Late Spring

Fruit Set

After successful pollination, the petals fall away and the ovary begins to swell into a tiny fruit. This is "fruit set," and it depends on favorable weather, adequate pollinator activity, and proper nutrition. Not all flowers will set fruit — trees naturally shed excess young fruit in a process called "June drop" to balance their crop load and ensure the remaining fruit reaches optimal size.

4
Early Summer

Cell Division

In the first weeks after fruit set, rapid cell division creates the foundation for the apple's final size. During this phase, each cell divides repeatedly, and the total number of cells in the fruit is largely determined. Adequate water, nutrients, and sunlight during this critical period directly influence the potential size and quality of the mature fruit.

5
Late Summer

Ripening

During ripening, the cells expand as they fill with water and sugars. Chlorophyll in the skin breaks down, revealing red, yellow, or orange pigments. Starch converts to sugar, organic acids mellow, and volatile aromatic compounds develop — creating the complex flavor and aroma that define a ripe apple. Ethylene gas naturally produced by the fruit triggers these cascading changes.

6
Autumn

Harvest

The moment of harvest is determined by measuring sugar content (Brix), firmness, and background color. Pick too early and the apple won't reach its full flavor potential; pick too late and it may be mealy. Skilled orchardists know each variety's optimal harvest window. Hand-picking remains common for premium fruit, while mechanical harvesters are used for processing apples destined for juice and sauce.

The Science of Apples

Beneath the surface lies a world of fascinating chemistry and biology.

🧬

Genetics & Breeding

The apple genome, fully sequenced in 2010, contains about 57,000 genes — roughly twice as many as the human genome. Apples are diploid organisms with 17 chromosomes. Each apple seed is genetically unique (except identical twins from the same seed), meaning every seedling is a potential new variety. This genetic diversity is both the apple's strength and the challenge facing breeders who must evaluate thousands of seedlings to find a superior new cultivar.

🌀

Ethylene & Ripening

Apples are climacteric fruits, meaning they continue to ripen after picking thanks to the plant hormone ethylene. A tiny amount of ethylene gas triggers a cascade of changes: starch converts to sugar, cell walls soften, and aromatic compounds are produced. This is why placing an apple in a paper bag with unripe fruit accelerates ripening. Commercially, ethylene is used to precisely control the timing of ripening in controlled-atmosphere storage facilities worldwide.

🧪

Phytochemistry

Apples contain over 100 identified phytochemicals, including quercetin, catechin, chlorogenic acid, and phloridzin. The skin alone contains a higher concentration of antioxidants than many so-called superfoods. These compounds have demonstrated anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, and neuroprotective properties in laboratory studies. The total antioxidant capacity of a single apple is equivalent to approximately 1,500 mg of vitamin C.

🐝

Pollination Biology

Apple pollination is a complex ecological dance. Most apple varieties are self-incompatible, requiring pollen from a different variety to produce fruit. Honeybees are the primary pollinators, visiting up to 5,000 flowers per day. Each blossom must receive adequate pollen to develop into a well-formed fruit. Poor pollination results in misshapen, "finger" apples. Orchardists often place managed beehives at rates of two to four hives per acre during bloom.

❄️

Chill Hours

Apple trees require a period of cold dormancy, measured in "chill hours" — cumulative hours below 45°F during winter. Different varieties have different requirements: some need only 500 hours, while others require 1,200 or more. Climate change is reducing chill hours in many traditional apple-growing regions, forcing growers to adapt by selecting low-chill varieties or employing chemical rest-breaking agents to ensure reliable bloom and fruit set.

🧰

Browning Chemistry

When you bite into an apple, you initiate an enzymatic browning reaction. The enzyme polyphenol oxidase (PPO), normally contained within cell vacuoles, reacts with phenolic compounds in the presence of oxygen to form melanins — the brown pigments you see on a cut surface. This is why lemon juice prevents browning: its ascorbic acid reduces the oxygen available for the reaction. Some modern apple varieties have been bred with reduced PPO activity for slower browning.

Cider & Beverages

The apple's versatility extends far beyond the kitchen counter into the world of drinks.

🍼

Fresh Juice

Fresh-pressed apple juice is a celebration of pure, unadulterated flavor. Made by crushing ripe apples and extracting the liquid, it retains the fruit's natural sweetness and a portion of its nutrients. A single glass of fresh apple juice typically requires two to three apples, and the flavor varies dramatically depending on the varieties used. Artisanal producers blend sweet and tart cultivars to achieve the perfect balance.

🍺

Hard Cider

Fermented apple cider, known as hard cider, has a history as long as the apple itself. The juice of bitter and bittersweet apple varieties is fermented with yeast, converting sugars into alcohol. Traditional English and French cider apples are specifically grown for their tannin and acid content, producing complex, wine-like beverages that range from bone-dry to off-dry. The craft cider movement has sparked a resurgence in pomology and traditional cider orchards.

🍺️

Perry & Sommeau

Perry, also known as pear cider, is produced by fermenting pear juice using the same principles as apple cider. A creamier, more subtle cousin of cider, perry benefits from the delicate aromatics of pear varieties. Calvados, meanwhile, is the celebrated apple brandy of Normandy, produced by double-distilling cider and aging it in oak barrels for years — producing a rich, complex spirit treasured by connoisseurs worldwide. These beverages demonstrate the astonishing range of what the apple can become.

The Seasons of the Orchard

Each season paints the apple orchard in its own unique colors and rhythms.

🌿

Spring

Spring is a time of awakening and promise. The orchard erupts in a breathtaking display of blossoms — petals of white and soft pink covering every branch. Bees hum busily among the flowers, orchestrating the miracle of pollination. Growers watch the weather anxiously, knowing a late frost can devastate the year's crop. The air is fragrant with the delicate scent of apple blossoms, a fleeting beauty that lasts barely two weeks.

🌞

Summer

Summer brings gentle growth and transformation. Tiny fruits swell upon the branches, gradually developing their shape and color. The orchard canopy thickens, providing shade and homes for countless birds and insects. It is a season of quiet anticipation — water must be managed carefully, pests monitored, and the trees nourished. By late summer, early varieties begin to blush, hinting at the abundance soon to come.

🍂

Autumn

Autumn is the grand climax of the apple year. Orchards glow with ripe fruit in every shade of red, gold, and green. The harvest becomes a community celebration — families gather to pick, press, and preserve. Leaves turn brilliant amber and crimson before falling to carpet the ground. The season is a sensory feast of crisp bites, sweet aromas, and the deep satisfaction of a year's labor come to fruition.

❄️

Winter

Winter blankets the orchard in stillness and rest. Bare branches etch elegant silhouettes against pale skies, while a blanket of snow protects the soil. The trees are dormant, gathering their strength for another cycle. It is a season of quiet preparation — of pruning, planning, and dreaming of the abundance to come. Beneath the soil, roots rest; within the wood, the promise of spring's blossoms patiently waits.

The Apple in Culture

Few fruits carry the symbolic weight that the apple has accumulated across human cultures.

🕊

Religion

The apple appears throughout religious and mythological traditions. In Judeo-Christian tradition, the forbidden fruit in the Garden of Eden is often depicted as an apple, though the Bible never specifies the fruit's identity — the association arose from Latin wordplay. In Greek mythology, golden apples featured in the judgment of Paris and the labors of Hercules. Norse mythology tells of Idun's golden apples, which granted immortality to the gods. The apple is also central to the Jewish celebration of Rosh Hashanah, where honey-dipped apples symbolize a sweet new year.

🔬️

Science

Apples have punctuated scientific history in memorable ways. Sir Isaac Newton's legendary observation of a falling apple allegedly inspired his laws of gravity, changing our understanding of the physical universe. The very first documented application of the scientific method involved apple cultivation. And of course, the apple's hardiness and predictability made it a beloved subject for genetic and horticultural research. The tree that supposedly inspired Newton was famously blown down in 1820 and propagated for preservation.

👑

Mythology

Beyond religion, the apple features prominently in folklore across the globe. In Celtic mythology, the apple tree stood at the boundary between worlds, and the Isle of Avalon — the "Isle of Apples" — was a mystical afterlife realm. Sowing an apple pip into a well was said to aid in love divination. In Slavic folklore, golden apples granted strength and youth. The apple has consistently represented knowledge, temptation, renewal, and the threshold between the mortal and the magical in countless traditions.

🎨

Art & Literature

From Renaissance masterpieces to contemporary verse, the apple has inspired artists and writers for centuries. The apple in still-life painting became a symbol of both abundance and mortality. Literary works from Shakespeare to Joyce have used the apple as a motif of temptation, knowledge, and simple goodness. Robert Frost's poetic imagery of orchards and apples evokes a nostalgic American pastoral. The apple's universal familiarity makes it an endlessly powerful metaphor across all artistic media.

History, Deep Dive

Exploring the pivotal eras that shaped the apple's relationship with humanity.

Ancient Rome

The Orchardists of Antiquity

The Romans elevated apple cultivation to an agricultural art form. Pliny the Elder's Natural History catalogued more than twenty distinct apple varieties grown throughout the empire, including early names we can still recognize today. Roman horticulturists mastered techniques that remain in use: grafting superior scions onto hardy rootstocks, pruning for vigor, and selecting varieties suited to specific climates. As Roman legions marched across Europe, they carried apple cuttings and seeds, spreading orchards to Britain and Gaul. These systematic Roman practices formed the technical foundation for all subsequent apple cultivation in the Western world.

Medieval Monasteries

The Keepers of the Orchard

During the medieval period, monastic gardens became the primary repositories of apple knowledge and genetic diversity. Benedictine and Cistercian monks maintained meticulous orchards, preserving varieties that might otherwise have been lost and developing new ones through careful selection. The monastery of Corbeil in France and the Abbey of Woburn in England were renowned for their orchards. Monks also pioneered the production of cider, recognizing that fermented apple juice could provide a safe, stable beverage during an era when water was often contaminated. Many of the cider apple varieties still grown in Normandy and Somerset trace their lineage directly to these monastic plantings.

American Frontier

Pioneers & the Apple

The apple traveled to the New World as an essential provision for settlers. By law, some early colonial settlements required landowners to plant apple trees. Apples provided cider (the common beverage of daily life), food through the winter, and vinegar for preservation. John Chapman — the legendary Johnny Appleseed — walked thousands of miles planting apple nurseries across the American frontier, ensuring that every homestead had access to this vital fruit. He sold seedlings for a few pennies each, and his gentle, eccentric character became part of American folklore. The apple's success in America spawned entirely new varieties adapted to local conditions.

Modern Breeding

The Science of Improvement

The 20th century transformed apple breeding from an art into a science. Universities and government programs established systematic breeding initiatives, crossing elite varieties and rigorously evaluating thousands of seedlings over decades. The Honeycrisp, developed at the University of Minnesota, exemplifies this patient approach — it took over 30 years from initial cross to commercial release. Modern breeders now employ genetic markers to accelerate selection, and new varieties like Cosmic Crisp and Jazz demonstrate the ongoing quest for improved flavor, texture, shelf life, and disease resistance. The future of the apple continues to be written in research orchards around the world.

Fun Facts

Astounding apple trivia revealed, one card at a time.

01

Apple Diversity

There are more than 7,500 known apple cultivars grown worldwide, ranging from marble-sized crabapples to apples as large as a large human head.

02

Buoyant Fruit

Apples float in water because they are roughly 25% air. This is why apples are harvested with "hydro-cooling" and why they bob in the classic game of apple-bobbing.

03

World's Oldest Tree

The oldest living apple tree on record is more than 500 years old, located in Kazakhstan, the fruit's ancestral homeland.

04

Long Storage

Apples can be stored for up to a year in controlled-atmosphere facilities that precisely regulate temperature, oxygen, and carbon dioxide levels to suspend ripening.

05

Genetic Lottery

Each apple seed grows into a genetically unique tree — no seed ever produces an apple identical to its parent. All commercial varieties are propagated by grafting.

06

Tea Time

Apple peels can be dried and steeped into a fragrant, caffeine-free tea that showcases the subtle aromas of the fruit.

07

Bee Power

A single apple blossom requires multiple bee visits to be adequately pollinated, and orchardists typically place 2–4 beehives per acre during bloom.

08

Cosmic Record

The Guinness World Record for the world's heaviest apple is 1.67 kilograms — roughly the size of a gallon of milk.

09

Apple Symbolism

The apple has been a symbol of love, temptation, knowledge, beauty, health, and immortality across cultures — more diverse meanings than perhaps any other fruit.

10

Grafted Identity

There are apple trees in New Zealand that are over a century old and still bear the exact same variety they did the day they were grafted.

11

Non-Browning

The Arctic Apple, first approved in 2015, was genetically engineered to produce less browning enzyme, so it stays white and crisp after slicing for much longer.

Water Content

A single apple is about 85% water, making it a wonderfully hydrating snack — and one reason it is so refreshing on a warm day.

Apple Diversity

There are more than 7,500 known apple cultivars grown worldwide, ranging from marble-sized crabapples to apples as large as a large human head.

02

Buoyant Fruit

Apples float in water because they are roughly 25% air. This is why apples are harvested with "hydro-cooling" and why they bob in the classic game of apple-bobbing.
